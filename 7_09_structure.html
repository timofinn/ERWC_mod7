Chiasmus
Reversing the grammatical structure of a phrase so that A-B becomes B-A.
Why it works: The flip surprises the ear — the audience hears where the sentence is going, then it turns. The line feels both inevitable and unexpected at the same time.
Step 1 — Find an example in the wild

Search for chiasmus in political speeches, literature, or song lyrics. It should make you do a double-take when you hear the flip. Don't use one I've shown you.

Write down: the example, where it's from, and where exactly the A-B flips to B-A.
✍ Notebook
Step 2 — Try it

Take a phrase and reverse the structure. Start simple: pick two words or concepts and build a sentence where the second half flips the first. Then try a second attempt — ideally one you'd actually want in your speech.

Hint: start with "Not A but B" → then flip it to "Not B but A" to find the shape.
✍ Notebook
Step 3 — Say it

Read it aloud. If the flip doesn't land, the structure might be off. Tighten the parallel.

Hypophora
Asking a question and immediately answering it yourself — you set the frame and fill it.
Why it works: It controls the room. The speaker decides what question matters and what the answer is — the audience follows because the question was already in their heads.
Step 1 — Find an example in the wild

Find hypophora in a speech, sermon, op-ed, or advertisement. The question should feel like the one the audience was already silently asking. Your example, not mine.

Write down: the example, where it's from, and what effect the self-answered question creates.
✍ Notebook
Step 2 — Try it

Ask the question your graduation audience is already thinking — something about what high school was for, or what comes next. Then answer it. Write two different versions with two different questions.

✍ Notebook
Step 3 — Say it

Read it aloud. The question should feel like it came from the audience, and the answer should feel like a gift back to them.

Parallelism
Repeating the same grammatical structure across a sentence or series of ideas to give them equal weight.
Why it works: Parallel structure makes a list of ideas feel like a single fist — each item has the same weight and rhythm, so the whole thing lands as one unified thought.
Step 1 — Find an example in the wild

Find a parallelism where the repeated grammatical structure makes a list feel like one unified punch. Find your own — don't use mine.

Write down: the example, where it's from, and what the repeated structure does to the rhythm.
✍ Notebook
Step 2 — Try it

Write three parallel phrases that all start the same grammatical way — same verb form, same preposition, same structure. Let them build. Then try a second set with a different opening structure.

✍ Notebook
Step 3 — Say it

Read it aloud. All three parts should feel like equal weight. If one of them drags or rushes, fix the structure until they all land the same way.

Draft Sprint

The only speech that doesn't work is the one that stays in your head. Open the page.
1
0–10 min

Expand your best Anaphora or Tricolon from this week into a full paragraph. Build out the rhythm. Let it breathe.

2
10–20 min
