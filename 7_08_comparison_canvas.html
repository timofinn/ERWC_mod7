Antithesis
Two contrasting ideas placed in parallel grammatical structure, side by side.
Why it works: The contrast forces the audience to hold two ideas at once — the tension between them creates the meaning. It's the space between the words that does the work.
Step 1 — Find an example in the wild

Search for antithesis in a speech, book, headline, or song — something that puts two contrasting ideas in parallel structure. Don't use one I've shown you.

Write down: the example, where it's from, and what the tension between the two sides creates.
✍ Notebook
Step 2 — Try it

Write your own antithesis using the "Not X, but Y" structure. Finish the sentence about your graduating class, your generation, or something you actually believe. Then try a second one on a different subject.

✍ Notebook
Step 3 — Say it

Read it out loud. The two halves should feel like they're pulling against each other. If they don't, sharpen the contrast.

Metaphor
Calling one thing another thing entirely — not a comparison, a direct statement of identity.
Why it works: A good metaphor creates an instant picture in the listener's mind that argument alone can't produce — it makes an abstract idea feel real and physical.
Step 1 — Find an example in the wild

Find a metaphor from a speech, poem, song, or book that actually surprises you — not a dead cliché like "life is a journey." It should make you see something differently. Find your own.

Write down: the example, where it's from, and what picture it creates in your head.
✍ Notebook
Step 2 — Try it

High school IS ____________. Pick one surprising, non-cliché object or thing and make the case — directly, not with "like" or "as." Then try a second one. Push yourself past the first answer that comes to mind.

The first idea is usually the cliché. The second one is usually better.
✍ Notebook
Step 3 — Say it

Read it aloud. Does it land visually? Would someone who heard it see something?

Analogy
An extended comparison developed across 3–4 sentences that builds an argument through a sustained parallel.
Why it works: The extended comparison locks the audience inside a logic they already understand — once they accept the analogy, the argument follows automatically.
Step 1 — Find an example in the wild

Find an analogy that actually builds over multiple sentences — not a one-liner. It should feel like an argument, not a decoration. Your find, not mine.

Write down: the example, where it's from, and what argument it's making.
✍ Notebook
Step 2 — Try it

Pick one object. High school IS that thing. Now make the case across 3–4 sentences — build the comparison out. Then try it with a second object. The extended version is harder than it looks.

✍ Notebook
Step 3 — Say it

Read it aloud. Does the comparison hold up from sentence to sentence? If it starts to break down, that's where you revise.
