Anaphora
Repetition of the same word or phrase at the beginning of successive lines or clauses.
Why it works: The repetition builds pressure in the listener's chest — by the third line, they're leaning forward waiting for the payoff.
Step 1 — Find an example in the wild

Search for a real example of anaphora in a speech, song, poem, or piece of writing. Not mine. Yours.

Write down: the example, where it's from, and why it works.
✍ Notebook
Step 2 — Try it

Write 3 lines starting with the same phrase. The topic is your choice — your class, your life, something you believe.

Try it once. Then try it again with a different opening phrase. Two attempts, minimum.
✍ Notebook
Step 3 — Say it

Read your best version aloud — to yourself, a partner, or the room. The ear catches what the eye misses.

Antistrophe
Repetition of the same word or phrase at the end of successive clauses.
Why it works: Ending on the same word feels like a door clicking shut — it signals the audience that something important just landed.
Step 1 — Find an example in the wild

Search for antistrophe in a speech, prayer, song, or political address. Find your own — don't use mine.

Write down: the example, where it's from, and what effect the repeated ending creates.
✍ Notebook
Step 2 — Try it

Write 3 lines that all end with the same word that matters most to you right now. Then try a second version with a different ending word.

✍ Notebook
Step 3 — Say it

Read your best version aloud. Notice where the repeated ending lands in your chest.

Tricolon
Three parallel items or phrases that escalate in intensity. The third one must land the hardest.
Why it works: The rule of three satisfies a pattern the brain craves — and the third item hits with the weight of everything that came before it.
Step 1 — Find an example in the wild

Find a real tricolon — in advertising, politics, literature, or music. The third item should clearly carry the most weight.

Write down: the example, where it's from, and why the third item lands hardest.
✍ Notebook
Step 2 — Try it

Write a tricolon built around your high school experience: one memory, one struggle, one victory. Make the third one land hardest. Then try a second version about something else entirely.

✍ Notebook
Step 3 — Say it

Read it out loud. If the third item doesn't feel like a gut punch, rewrite it until it does.
